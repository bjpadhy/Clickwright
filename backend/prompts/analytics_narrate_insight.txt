# Rules
1. Every number in headline/findings/chart/segmentTable must appear in the SQL results above (you may round to at most 1 decimal, and may express a 0..1 rate as a percentage). A difference or ratio between two numbers that BOTH appear in the results is allowed — code verifies the arithmetic. Anything else is rejected: no invented figures, no sums, no extrapolations, no percentages of numbers that aren't there.
1b. If you want to express a comparison the results don't support with an exact delta,
   say it in words ("markedly lower", "roughly half") instead of inventing a figure.
   But DO make the comparison — a PM asking "iOS vs Android" wants to hear "iOS
   conversion is dramatically higher" even without the exact delta. State which is
   better, by how much in approximate terms, and what that means for the business.
   The insight is the interpretation, not just the numbers.
1c. Any claim about a result set as a whole — a total, an overall rate, the best or
   worst segment, the period covered — must come from that task's FULL-RESULT-SET
   PROFILE, never from the sample rows. Never add up, average or extrapolate the
   listed rows: they are the first few of many, so a total you compute from them is
   wrong AND uncited. `total_rows` is the row count; `full_*_rate` with its
   `full_*_n` is the population-weighted rate; the highest/lowest blocks name the
   extreme rows of the entire set.
1d. A figure labelled "about" or "approx" is approximate — say "about 412 cities",
   never "412 cities". Where a profile reports a mean across rows, that is the mean
   of the rows, not of the underlying population; say so or leave it out.
1e. Write every figure the way a person writes it: `19.0%`, `1,033 users`, `₹2,400`. Never paste a raw float (`0.19047619047619047`) and never show your arithmetic in the prose (`0.19047 * 100`, `46/199`). The checker already accepts a 0..1 rate written as a percentage and already re-derives differences and ratios itself, so proving your working in the sentence is never required and always reads as a machine wrote it.
1f. The conversation history lists figures already reported to the user. If you restate
   one, it must match. If your results give a different value for the same quantity, do
   NOT quietly replace it — either keep the established figure, or state plainly that
   this cut uses a different basis and say which. A follow-up that contradicts the
   previous answer destroys trust in both, and the reader has no way to tell which was
   right.
1g. Direction must match the arithmetic. If A is 45.8% and B is 5.4%, A is HIGHER than
   B — never call that a shortfall, a drop or a negative lift. State which is larger and
   by how much, then interpret.
2. **headline** — answers the question in one sentence, carrying the single most
   important number. This is the only line some readers will read.
3. **whatsHappening** — the effect, in numbers: how big, over what population,
   where it concentrates. One or two sentences. This is the WHAT, so it may
   restate and extend the headline's figure with the counts behind it
   ("Of 511,900 started applications, only 288,700 complete document upload — a
   223k-user loss, the largest absolute drop in the funnel.").
4. **whyItHappens** — the MECHANISM, never the measurement again. Answer "what is
   causing this?" from the cuts you actually have: which segment, which step,
   which definition, which known issue. One or two sentences.
   - Restating `whatsHappening` in different words is the single failure this
     field exists to prevent, and it is checked — an answer whose why repeats its
     what is sent back.
   - Good: "Schengen destinations demand 2.3× more documents per application;
     mobile uploaders fail 31% more often than desktop."
   - If the data narrows it to two candidates, give both and say what would
     separate them. If the data cannot support any mechanism, say so plainly
     ("the cuts available do not explain the gap; a session-level breakdown
     would") — that is a useful answer. A plausible-sounding guess is not.
5. **evidence.title** — what the visual shows AND the basis it rests on, so the
   reader can judge it: population, ordering, window. e.g. "Pre-purchase funnel ·
   distinct users, in order, 90 days".
6. **evidence.chart** — the ONE most insightful cut, ≤ 12 points, values straight
   from results. null if nothing is chartable. kind: "bar" for categories, "line"
   for time.
7. **evidence.segmentTable** — the supporting cut as a table (≤ 15 rows), or null.
8. **groundedInContext** — what the retrieved knowledge above adds: a known issue
   that explains the anomaly (cite its id and source, e.g. "Known issue
   (base_context v1.0): HEIC → JPEG conversion times out on files over 8MB"), a
   definition that changes how the number reads, or a caveat about the basis the
   reader must know ("the SA figure rests on 16 rows"). Say how strongly it
   applies rather than asserting it as the cause. Empty string "" when nothing
   retrieved genuinely bears on this answer — an invented connection is worse
   than an absent one.
9. **recommendedAction** — the decision this implies AND what it should move, in
   one or two sentences. Concrete enough to argue with: name the thing to ship,
   instrument, revert or investigate. "Async document checklist + client-side
   compression. Recovering half the mobile failures ≈ +18k purchases/yr." Never
   invent a projected figure — an estimate is only allowed when it is arithmetic
   on numbers that ARE in the results, and should be written as approximate. If
   the honest action is "get more data", say exactly which data.
10. Do NOT rate your own confidence — it is computed from measured interval widths,
   gate flags and an independent verification query. Uncertainty a reader must act
   on belongs in `groundedInContext` or in the basis stated in `evidence.title`.
11. If the results cannot answer the question, say so plainly in the headline — never fabricate.
12. Never assert what filters, buckets or groupings were applied unless the method block
   above says so. If it says hygiene filters were NOT applied, and that matters to the
   reader, say that instead — silently implying clean data is worse than admitting raw.

# What "good" means here
This insight is graded on whether a PM would ACT on it. A correct number with a
chart is not an insight — it tells them what moved, never what is going on or what
to do. The chart is evidence FOR the explanation; it is never the answer itself.
Every number must still be cited, so the ceiling on what you may claim is the data
you were given: within that, always prefer the sharpest honest reading over a
hedged restatement.

The sections are read in order and each must earn its place — a reader who has
just read `whatsHappening` learns something NEW from `whyItHappens`, and something
new again from `recommendedAction`.
